A Visit to the Sanjay Sharma Museum, Jaipur

12 October 2026 · Dominik Wujastyk

  • manuscripts
  • Jaipur
  • Rajasthan
  • Sanjay-Sharma-Museum
  • rasaśāstra

On 11 October 2026, Jason Birch and I made our first visit to the Shree Sanjay Sharma Museum and Research Institute in Jaipur. The museum stands on the Amer Road at Gujarghati, Parasrampuri, looking across the lake to the Jal Mahal. We came with two questions in mind: what manuscripts of the Suśrutasaṃhitā does the collection hold, and are there illustrated manuscripts on alchemy (rasaśāstra)?

We were warmly received by Chaitanya Sharma, grandson of the museum’s founder, Ram Kṛpālu Sharma, who took us through the galleries.

A collection that began with four manuscripts

The museum’s origins are literary. In the 1950s Ram Kṛpālu Sharma, who began his career as a schoolteacher, struggled to get his own writings published: a book of prose and verse, Prāñjali, appeared only after great difficulty, and a second, Vāṅmayī, never saw print for lack of funds. That experience led him, from 1955, to start collecting and preserving manuscripts — beginning with just four. The collection was first known as the Sharma Grantha Saṅgrahālaya. After the untimely death of his younger son, Sanjay Sharma, in 1982, it took its present name. With his wife Padmavati Sharma and the wider family, the founder went on to build the museum and library collections, and the institution remains a private, family-run foundation.

The family now describes the collection as numbering around a hundred thousand manuscripts, together with artefacts that illustrate their subjects. According to the Museums of India listing, the holdings span more than 200 subjects in some 16 languages and scripts — among them Brāhmī, Śāradā, Tibetan, Arabic, Persian, Tirhutā, Bengali, Telugu, Gujarati, Oriya, Gurmukhi and Devanāgarī — on palm leaf, birch bark and paper. Subject areas named include Vedic literature, jyotiṣa, tantra, dhanurveda, āyurveda and medicine, and “alchemy and chemistry”.

The galleries

We were stunned by the extent and quality of the manuscripts, paintings and artefacts on display in the museum’s numerous spacious, marble-floored galleries. Any visitor to Jaipur with an interest in Indian history and culture should not leave without seeing this extraordinary collection.

The museum is laid out in several galleries: glass painting and porcelain; miniature painting (fifteenth to nineteenth centuries, from Jaipur, Kishangarh, Kota, Bundi, Bikaner, Nathdwara and elsewhere); textiles and costumes, largely from the Jaipur court; cloth paintings and picchvāīs; an art gallery; illustrated manuscripts; and the manuscript library itself.

Among the illustrated material is a late nineteenth-century cosmological painting from Jaipur depicting the fourteen worlds (caturdaśabhuvana), from a series catalogued as the Viśvavijñāna-citrāvalī (1895). The tantra holdings include yantras such as an eighteenth-century Śani-yantra from Marwar.

Photography is strictly prohibited in the museum, so we are unfortunately unable to illustrate what we saw on display.

What we were looking for

The Suśrutasaṃhitā

As this website explains, our project is focussed on the textual and doctrinal differences between The Compendium as it existed in the earliest manuscript of 878 CE and the later “vulgate” version that was edited and reshaped by medieval commentators over the centuries. Manuscripts matter to the project, helping us to understand the history of this foundational work of medicine, and we are trying to include as many manuscripts as possible in our research.

During our visit on 11 and 12 October, we saw a Suśrutasaṃhitā manuscript on display, but unfortunately we were not permitted to examine it. It is numbered MS Jaipur Sanjay Sharma Museum RN4MSS 4720. The single leaf on display had text that appeared to be a commentary on the main work, in a section about the Āyurvedic concept of “appropriateness” (Skt. sātmya). One passage cited the author Caraka. On the second day of our visit, Shri Tilak Sharma, the Museum’s Director, shared with us a photocopy of the last two folios of the manuscript. They showed it to be the ending of the last chapter of the Uttaratantra, with the final “Candraṭa” verse that we have discussed in another blog post. We hope the Museum will see a way to share a digital image of the whole manuscript with us in due course.

Illustrated rasaśāstra

Professor Dagmar Wujastyk has been researching the history of Indian alchemy for several years. She has published several research articles on the history of rasaśāstra and on illustrated rasaśāstra manuscripts (Wujastyk, 2024). Her most recent book on the history of Indian alchemy was published by Oxford University Press in 2025 (Wujastyk, 2025). She had asked me to look out for illustrated alchemical manuscripts, and I was delighted to see a richly illustrated copy of an unnamed rasaśāstra text in bhāṣā in one of the display cabinets. However, photography was not allowed and, since manuscripts are not at present being made available for study, research has to stop there for the time being.

Next steps

The Director, Shri Tilak Sharma, said that there was no handlist of the 125,000 manuscripts in the collection, so it is difficult to know whether there are more copies of the Suśrutasaṃhitā, the Rasaratnākara, or other interesting works in the collection. When I met the Museum’s founder, Shri Ram Kṛpālu Sharma, in 2011, he asserted confidently that there was a strong Āyurveda component in the Museum’s manuscript library, and that it included manuscripts of the Carakasaṃhitā and other classic works. This seems very likely.

The Museum is in discussion with Gyan Bharatam (see our earlier post), and it is to be hoped that a mutually beneficial MOU can be signed in future that will help make the library collection accessible to research scholars.

Visiting

The museum is open daily from 9:00 am to 5:30 pm; allow one to two hours. Photography is not permitted inside. Contact details are on the museum’s website.
