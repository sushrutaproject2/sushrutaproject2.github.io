In early October 2026 I visited the Āśā Saphū Kuthi in Kathmandu, a library best known for its Nepāl Bhāṣā (Newar) books and manuscripts, to look for manuscripts of the _Suśrutasaṃhitā_.

## Finding the library

It is impossible to see the library building from the main road (Cittadhar Mārg), which is lined with two-storey shopfronts. Between two of the shops, a doorway with a carved wooden architrave opens onto a corridor that leads to a courtyard behind them. High above the doorway, and hard to see from street level, a sign in [Rañjanā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranjana_script#) script reads _Āśā Saphūkuthi_.

As one emerges from the corridor, a three-storeyed library building in traditional Newar style towers above. Over the porch the name is set in gilt letters in the same script, with "The Āśā Archives" in English beside it.

## A morning with the catalogue

The library opens at 11 am. The front door is not locked but needs a good push; not knowing this, I waited outside until someone who did arrived. In the morning the library is staffed by an assistant, who showed me the printed catalogue of manuscripts {% cite saky-2000 %}. It is arranged by subject, so I went straight to the medical (_vaidya_) section.

The collection of medical works is fairly impressive. The section has nearly two hundred entries, many of them works in Nepāl Bhāṣā or bilingual Sanskrit–Nepāl Bhāṣā texts on diseases, diagnosis, pulse, remedies and mantras.

## Viewing the scans

The librarian does not arrive until after 3 pm, so my visit was punctuated by a long lunch at a nearby restaurant. I got the impression that the whole manuscript collection has been digitised and is stored on hard drives kept in a locked cabinet. The librarian, who holds the key, did indeed arrive at 3 pm. She was very friendly and let me look at whatever manuscript scans I wanted, at a fee of 100 NPR an hour on the library's computer.

## What I found

Among the Sanskrit medical works, two stand out: a complete manuscript of the relatively rare _Mādhavacikitsā_ (DPN 1599), and an incomplete one of Ravigupta's _Siddhasāra_ (DPN 1660, chapters 1–8). There is also a damaged fragment of an illustrated work on horse medicine (_aśvacikitsā_) with four hand-painted illustrations of horses. A second veterinary text, a _Śālihotra_ in Sanskrit and Maithilī, is also listed in the catalogue.

Unfortunately for us, there were no _Suśrutasaṃhitā_ manuscripts.

## Practical notes for visitors

- The library opens at 11 am every day except Saturday. Push the front door firmly.
- The librarian, who controls access to the scans, arrives after 3 pm.
- Viewing scans costs 100 NPR an hour.
- There is a photocopier. Digital images of manuscripts may perhaps be purchased, but I did not broach that topic.

For anyone working on Sanskrit or Newar medicine, the Āśā Saphū Kuthi repays the afternoon's wait.

## Background: the library and its collection

The Āśā Saphū Kuthi (आशा सफू कुथि, "Āśā's book house"), also called the Āśā Archives, is a free public library across Cittadhar Mārg from the Raktakālī temple. As Rhishav Sapkota describes {% cite sapk-2021 %}, it is named after Āśā Mān Siṃh Kansakar (1916–1990), founder of the Nepāl Bhāṣā publisher Cwāsā Pāsā, who resolved that Nepal should keep its own manuscripts rather than see them sold abroad. His son, the politician Prem Bahadur Kansakar, carried the project forward, and the American scholar Ian Alsop gave almost 500 manuscripts. The present building, funded by the Toyota Foundation, opened on 7 December 1987.

The early collection holds over 7,000 manuscripts from the fifteenth to the nineteenth century, in Sanskrit, Nepāl Bhāṣā and Nepali, on religion, poetics, astronomy, astrology and medicine. The catalogue I used, _Āśā Saphūkuthiyā Saphū Dhalaḥ: A Short Catalogue of Āśā Archives_, was compiled by Rājā Śākya and published in N.S. 1120 (2000 CE) {% cite saky-2000 %}; each entry gives a DPN number, script, language, date, size, folios and material. An earlier catalogue of the library's inscriptions and documents appeared in 1991 {% cite vaid-1991 %}.

The scans I saw are the fruit of several digitization projects: a first phase led by Shucho Takaoka in 1987; the University of Hamburg's _Preserving the Written Heritage of Nepal_ (2018–2022); and the [Hill Museum & Manuscript Library](https://hmml.org/collections/repositories/nepal/%C4%81%C5%9B%C4%81-saph%C5%ABkuthidpn/)'s digitization of 1,332 items from the "new collection" (2019–2024, DPN 07172–08121).  Many manuscript scans are now available through the [Āśā Archives page at Archive.org](https://archive.org/details/asa-archives?tab=collection).
